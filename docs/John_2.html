3:1
[翻訳注] 「残っていない（left）」という言葉はギリシャ語のテキストにはありませんが、文脈から含意されています。[解説] 「彼らにはぶどう酒が残っていない」。この奇跡の背景について、J・D・M・デレットは、古代近東の婚礼における強い互酬性の要素を指摘しています。状況によっては、適切な婚礼の贈り物を用意しなかった男性に対して法的措置をとることが可能でした。ここでの新郎とその家族は、招待客に十分なもてなしを提供できなかったことで、法的・金銭的責任を問われる可能性がありました（「水からぶどう酒へ」BZ 7 [1963]: 80-97）。マリアは奇跡を求めていたのでしょうか？ これ以前にイエスが奇跡を行ったという証拠はありません（もっともこれは沈黙からの議論に過ぎませんが）。マリアは単に状況を報告していただけだとか、（カルヴァンが考えたように）客たちに神的な勧告を与えて新郎の恥を和らげるようイエスに求めただけだと言う人々もいます。しかし、その言葉と、第4節におけるイエスの返答は、それ以上の意味合いを含んでいるようです。それまでの数日間の出来事の目撃者であったか、少なくともそれを知っていたマリアが、息子の公生涯が始まっていることを知っていたとしても不自然ではありません。彼女はまた、彼の誕生を取り巻く超自然的な出来事や、天使の預言的言葉、そしてイエスの宮での奉献の際におけるシメオンとアンナの預言も知っていました。一言で言えば、彼女にはイエスをメシアと信じる十分な理由があり、今や公の宣教が始まったのです。このような文脈において、彼女の願いはより重大な意味を持つように思われます。
[翻訳注] The word “left” is not in the Greek text but is implied.[解説] They have no wine left. On the backgrounds of this miracle J. D. M. Derrett pointed out among other things the strong element of reciprocity about weddings in the Ancient Near East. It was possible in certain circumstances to take legal action against the man who failed to provide an appropriate wedding gift. The bridegroom and family here might have been involved in a financial liability for failing to provide adequately for their guests (“Water into Wine,” BZ 7 [1963]: 80-97). Was Mary asking for a miracle? There is no evidence that Jesus had worked any miracles prior to this (although this is an argument from silence). Some think Mary was only reporting the situation, or (as Calvin thought) asking Jesus to give some godly exhortations to the guests and thus relieve the bridegroom’s embarrassment. But the words, and the reply of Jesus in v. 4, seem to imply more. It is not inconceivable that Mary, who had probably been witness to the events of the preceding days, or at least was aware of them, knew that her son’s public career was beginning. She also knew the supernatural events surrounding his birth, and the prophetic words of the angel, and of Simeon and Anna in the temple at Jesus’ dedication. In short, she had good reason to believe Jesus to be the Messiah, and now his public ministry had begun. In this kind of context, her request does seem more significant.

4:2
[解説] 「婦人よ（Woman）」という表現は、女性に対するイエスの通常かつ礼儀正しい呼びかけ方です（マタイ15:28、ルカ13:12、ヨハネ4:21; 8:10; 19:26; 20:15）。しかし、息子が母親に対してこの用語を使うのは異例です。ヘブル語（またはアラム語）およびギリシャ語の習慣では、息子は修飾する形容詞や称号を用いるのが普通でした。ここでのイエスのこの言葉の使用には何か意味があるのでしょうか？ おそらくそれは、公生涯に踏み出したイエスと彼の母との間に新しい関係が存在するようになったことを示しています。彼はもはや、あるいは第一義的に彼女の息子ではなく、「人の子」でした。これは、十字架の場面における19:26での同じ用語の使用によっても示唆されており、そこでは愛する弟子がマリアの「新しい」息子として「与えられています」。
[解説] The term Woman is Jesus’ normal, polite way of addressing women (Matt 15:28, Luke 13:12; John 4:21; 8:10; 19:26; 20:15). But it is unusual for a son to address his mother with this term. The custom in both Hebrew (or Aramaic) and Greek would be for a son to use a qualifying adjective or title. Is there significance in Jesus’ use here? It probably indicates that a new relationship existed between Jesus and his mother once he had embarked on his public ministry. He was no longer or primarily only her son, but the “Son of Man.” This is also suggested by the use of the same term in 19:26 in the scene at the cross, where the beloved disciple is “given” to Mary as her “new” son.
4:3
[翻訳注] 原文のギリシャ語では「婦人よ、私とあなたに何があるというのか（What to me and to you?）」となっており、これはイディオムである。フレーズ「τί ἐμοὶ καὶ σοί, γύναι (ti emoi kai soi, gunai)」はセム語に起源を持つ。旧約聖書における同等のヘブル語表現には主に2つの意味があった。(1) ある人が不当に別の人の邪魔をしている時、被害を受けた側が「私とあなたに何があるか（私に何の関わりがあるか）」と言って、「あなたが私にこのようなことをするよう、私はあなたに何をしたというのか」という意味を表す場合（士師記11:12、歴代志下35:21、列王紀上17:18）。(2) 自分には関係のないことに巻き込まれるよう求められた人が、求めてきた相手に対して「私とあなたに何があるか」と言う場合で、「それはあなたの問題であって、どうして私が関わらなければならないのか」という意味を表す場合（列王紀上3:13、ホセア14:8）。(1)は敵対心を含意し、(2)は単に関与の拒否を含意する。文脈によりよく適合するものとして、ここでの意味はほぼ間違いなく単に関与の拒否（切り離し）と理解されるべきである（一部のギリシャ教父はこの発言をマリアへの非難と解釈したが、そのような非難であるとは考えにくい）。
[翻訳注] Grk “Woman, what to me and to you?” (an idiom). The phrase τί ἐμοὶ καὶ σοί, γύναι (ti emoi kai soi, gunai) is Semitic in origin. The equivalent Hebrew expression in the Old Testament had two basic meanings: (1) When one person was unjustly bothering another, the injured party could say “What to me and to you?” meaning, “What have I done to you that you should do this to me?” (Judg 11:12, 2 Chr 35:21, 1 Kgs 17:18). (2) When someone was asked to get involved in a matter he felt was no business of his, he could say to the one asking him, “What to me and to you?” meaning, “That is your business, how am I involved?” (2 Kgs 3:13, Hos 14:8). Option (1) implies hostility, while option (2) implies merely disengagement. Mere disengagement is almost certainly to be understood here as better fitting the context (although some of the Greek Fathers took the remark as a rebuke to Mary, such a rebuke is unlikely).
4:4
[翻訳注] 原文のギリシャ語では「私の時（my hour）」となっており、イエスの十字架刑と御父のもとへの帰還の時を指している。[解説] 「時」と訳されたギリシャ語（ὥρα, hōra）は、ヨハネ2:4; 4:21, 23; 5:25, 28, 29; 7:30; 8:20; 12:23, 27; 13:1; 16:25; 17:1に現れる。これは、イエスがこの世を去り、御父のもとへ戻るべき彼の生涯における特別な期間を指す（13:1）。それは人の子が栄光を受ける時である（17:1）。これは彼の苦難、死、復活（および昇天――ただし最後のものはヨハネでは強調されていない）を通して成し遂げられる。ヨハネ7:30および8:20は、イエスの逮捕と死が含まれていることを暗示している。ヨハネ12:23および17:1は、御子の栄光化に言及しており、復活と昇天が「時」の一部として含まれていることを暗示している。ヨハネ2:4における母に対するイエスの発言は、この自己顕示の時がまだ到来していないこと、すなわち彼のメシアとしてのアイデンティティがまだ公に明らかにされるべきではないことを示している。
[翻訳注] Grk “my hour” (referring to the time of Jesus’ crucifixion and return to the Father).[解説] The Greek word translated time (ὥρα, Jwra) occurs in John 2:4; 4:21, 23; 5:25, 28, 29; 7:30; 8:20; 12:23, 27; 13:1; 16:25; and 17:1. It is a reference to the special period in Jesus’ life when he was to leave this world and return to the Father (13:1); the hour when the Son of man is glorified (17:1). This is accomplished through his suffering, death, resurrection (and ascension – though this last is not emphasized by John). John 7:30 and 8:20 imply that Jesus’ arrest and death are included. John 12:23 and 17:1, referring to the glorification of the Son, imply that the resurrection and ascension are included as part of the “hour.” In John 2:4 Jesus’ remark to his mother indicates that the time for this self-manifestation has not yet arrived; his identity as Messiah is not yet to be publicly revealed.

6:2
[翻訳注] 原文のギリシャ語では「メトレテス2つまたは3つを収める（holding two or three metretes）」（約75〜115リットル）とある。めったなことではないが、各壺には2つまたは3つのメトレテス（μετρηταί）が入っていた。1メトレテス（μετρητής）は約9ガロン（40リットル）であり、したがって各壺には18〜27ガロン（80〜120リットル）が入っており、液体全体の総量は108〜162ガロン（480〜720リットル）であった。[解説] 重要なことに、これらの壺にはユダヤ人の清めの礼式（浄化の儀式）のための水が入っていた。ユダヤ人の儀式上の清めの水が、新しいメシア的時代のぶどう酒となったのである。このぶこう酒はまた、ヨハネ特有の二重の意味のスタイルにならって、主の晩餐のぶどう酒を指している可能性もある。多くの学者がこれをほのめかしているが、直接の文脈の中にこれを強制するものはないようである。むしろ、ヨハネの福音書全体におけるサクラメント（秘跡）への言及を、ある解釈者がどれほど頻繁に見出すかに深く関わっているように思われる。
[翻訳注] Grk “holding two or three metretes” (about 75 to 115 liters). Each of the pots held 2 or 3 μετρηταί (metrhtai). A μετρητῆς (metrhths) was about 9 gallons (40 liters); thus each jar held 18-27 gallons (80-120 liters) and the total volume of liquid involved was 108-162 gallons (480-720 liters).[解説] Significantly, these jars held water for Jewish ceremonial washing (purification rituals). The water of Jewish ritual purification has become the wine of the new messianic age. The wine may also be, after the fashion of Johannine double meanings, a reference to the wine of the Lord’s Supper. A number have suggested this, but there does not seem to be anything in the immediate context which compels this; it seems more related to how frequently a given interpreter sees references to the sacraments in John’s Gospel as a whole.

14:1
[解説] John 2:14-22. Does John’s account of the temple cleansing describe the same event as the synoptic gospels describe, or a separate event? The other accounts of the cleansing of the temple are Matt 21:12-13; Mark 11:15-17; and Luke 19:45-46. None are as long as the Johannine account. The fullest of the synoptic accounts is Mark’s. John’s account differs from Mark’s in the mention of sheep and oxen, the mention of the whip of cords, the Greek word κερματιστῆς (kermatisths) for money changer (the synoptics use κολλυβιστῆς [kollubisths], which John mentions in 2:15), the scattering of the coins (2:15), and the command by Jesus, “Take these things away from here!” The word for overturned in John is ἀναστρεφω (anastrefw), while Matthew and Mark use καταστρεφω (katastrefw; Luke does not mention the moneychangers at all). The synoptics all mention that Jesus quoted Isa 56:7 followed by Jer 7:11. John mentions no citation of scripture at all, but says that later the disciples remembered Ps 69:9. John does not mention, as does Mark, Jesus’ prohibition on carrying things through the temple (i.e., using it for a shortcut). But the most important difference is one of time: In John the cleansing appears as the first great public act of Jesus’ ministry, while in the synoptics it is virtually the last. The most common solution of the problem, which has been endlessly discussed among NT scholars, is to say there was only one cleansing, and that it took place, as the synoptics record it, at the end of Jesus’ ministry. In the synoptics it appears to be the event that finalized the opposition of the high priest, and precipitated the arrest of Jesus. According to this view, John’s placing of the event at the opening of Jesus’ ministry is due to his general approach; it was fitting ‘theologically’ for Jesus to open his ministry this way, so this is the way John records it. Some have overstated the case for one cleansing and John’s placing of it at the opening of Jesus’ public ministry, however. For example W. Barclay stated: “John, as someone has said, is more interested in the truth than in the facts. He was not interested to tell men when Jesus cleansed the Temple; he was supremely interested in telling men that Jesus did cleanse the Temple” (John [DSBS], 94). But this is not the impression one gets by a reading of John’s Gospel: The evangelist seems to go out of his way to give details and facts, including notes of time and place. To argue as Barclay does that John is interested in truth apart from the facts is to set up a false dichotomy. Why should one have to assume, in any case, that there could have been only one cleansing of the temple? This account in John is found in a large section of nonsynoptic material. Apart from the work of John the Baptist – and even this is markedly different from the references in the synoptics – nothing else in the first five chapters of John’s Gospel is found in any of the synoptics. It is certainly not impossible that John took one isolated episode from the conclusion of Jesus’ earthly ministry and inserted it into his own narrative in a place which seemed appropriate according to his purposes. But in view of the differences between John and the synoptics, in both wording and content, as well as setting and time, it is at least possible that the event in question actually occurred twice (unless one begins with the presupposition that the Fourth Gospel is nonhistorical anyway). In support of two separate cleansings of the temple, it has been suggested that Jesus’ actions on this occasion were not permanent in their result, and after (probably) 3 years the status quo in the temple courts had returned to normal. And at this time early in Jesus’ ministry, he was virtually unknown. Such an action as he took on this occasion would have created a stir, and evoked the response John records in 2:18-22, but that is probably about all, especially if Jesus’ actions met with approval among part of the populace. But later in Jesus’ ministry, when he was well-known, and vigorously opposed by the high-priestly party in Jerusalem, his actions might have brought forth another, harsher response. It thus appears possible to argue for two separate cleansings of the temple as well as a single one relocated by John to suit his own purposes. Which then is more probable? On the whole, more has been made of the differences between John’s account and the synoptic accounts than perhaps should have been. After all, the synoptic accounts also differ considerably from one another, yet few scholars would be willing to posit four cleansings of the temple as an explanation for this. While it is certainly possible that the author did not intend by his positioning of the temple cleansing to correct the synoptics’ timing of the event, but to highlight its significance for the course of Jesus’ ministry, it still appears somewhat more probable that John has placed the event he records in the approximate period of Jesus’ public ministry in which it did occur, that is, within the first year or so of Jesus’ public ministry. The statement of the Jewish authorities recorded by the author (this temple has been under construction for forty-six years) would tend to support an earlier rather than a later date for the temple cleansing described by John, since 46 years from the beginning of construction on Herod’s temple in ca. 19 b.c. (the date varies somewhat in different sources) would be around a.d. 27. This is not conclusive proof, however.
